### 1. 7 Essential Food Idioms · 음식 관련 핵심 관용구 7선 (24:12–28:53)

**David:** Oh, now it is time for one of my favorite parts: food idioms and expressions. Because English has so many of them, and they come up all the time.

**Alice:** All the time in everyday conversation, in movies, in books, at work, everywhere. And if you don't know them, they can be very confusing. "That's not my cup of tea." What does that have to do with cups? With tea?

**David:** Nothing. It is not literally about tea. That is the beauty and the challenge of idioms. So, let's learn the most important food idioms in English with clear explanations and natural examples.

**Alice:** Idiom number one: "not my cup of tea."

**David:** "Not my cup of tea." It means not something I enjoy, not my preference, not my taste. "How was the horror movie?" "Honestly, not really my cup of tea. I prefer comedies."

**Alice:** "Not my cup of tea." It is not a negative judgment of the thing itself, just a personal preference.

**David:** Personal preference. "Horror movies are great, just not my cup of tea." You respect the thing, but it is not for you. "Not my cup of tea." Say it. "Not my cup of tea."

**Alice:** Idiom number two: "spill the beans."

**David:** "Spill the beans." This one is fun. It means to reveal a secret, to tell something that was supposed to be private. "Come on, spill the beans. What did he say?" "She spilled the beans about the surprise party. Now everyone knows."

**Alice:** "Spill the beans." To reveal a secret, to accidentally or intentionally tell something you should not. "Don't spill the beans. It's supposed to be a surprise." "Spill the beans." Say it. "Spill the beans."

**David:** Idiom number three: "the best thing since sliced bread."

**Alice:** Huh? This one is very British originally. "The best thing since sliced bread." It means something is the best, the greatest new thing. "This new app is the best thing since sliced bread. It organizes everything."

**David:** Because sliced bread, when it was invented, was considered so amazing, so convenient, so revolutionary.

**Alice:** Revolutionary. Before sliced bread, you had to cut your own bread every time. Huh? Can you imagine such hard work?

**David:** So sliced bread became the symbol of a great invention. And now anything amazing is "the best thing since sliced bread." "That new coffee machine at work is the best thing since sliced bread."

**Alice:** Priorities. Idiom number four: "bite off more than you can chew."

**David:** "Bite off more than you can chew." What a great image. You took a bite too big for your mouth.

**Alice:** Too big for your mouth. And the meaning: you took on more responsibilities or tasks than you can handle. "I agreed to lead three projects at work this month. I think I really bit off more than I could chew."

**David:** "Don't bite off more than you can chew. Take it one step at a time."

**Alice:** One step at a time. Good advice in both cooking and life. "Bite off more than you can chew." It means be careful, know your limits.

**David:** Know your limits in food, in work, in life. Idiom number five: "a piece of cake."

**Alice:** "A piece of cake." One of the most well-known idioms in English. It means something is very easy. "How was the test?" "Oh, it was a piece of cake. I finished in 10 minutes."

**David:** "A piece of cake." Easy, simple, no problem, no stress. "Don't worry about the interview. You'll be great. It'll be a piece of cake." "A piece of cake." Say it. "A piece of cake."

**Alice:** Easy, delicious, and a piece of cake. Idiom number six: "food for thought."

**David:** "Food for thought." This is a beautiful one. It means something that gives you ideas to think about, something worth considering. "That's interesting. It gives me food for thought." "Her speech was real food for thought. I've been thinking about it all day."

**Alice:** "Food for thought." Mental food. Ideas that nourish your mind the way food nourishes your body.

**David:** Nourish, feed, sustain. "That book was real food for thought." "Food for thought." Say it. "Food for thought."

**Alice:** And idiom number seven: "in a nutshell."

**David:** "In a nutshell." It means to summarize briefly, to give the short version, the essential information. "In a nutshell, the project is behind schedule and we need more resources."

**Alice:** "Can you explain what happened?" "In a nutshell, I was late, I missed the meeting, and the boss was not happy."

**David:** "In a nutshell." Short, concise, the summary. We use "in a nutshell" to give a brief overview, like fitting everything inside a tiny nutshell.

**Alice:** A tiny nutshell. All the important information compressed into a small space. "In a nutshell." Say it. "In a nutshell."

### 2. Vocabulary Review · 핵심 어휘·구문 복습 (28:53–31:06)

**David:** Vocabulary review time. Let's go through today's most important words and phrases. Nice and slow, nice and clear. Repeat after us. Out loud.

**Alice:** Out loud. Always out loud, not in your head. Your mouth needs practice, too. Remember day three: speaking muscles.

**David:** Speaking muscles. Exactly. Let's go. Word one: delicious. Delicious. The most important food compliment. "This is absolutely delicious." Delicious. Say it. Delicious.

**Alice:** Word two: bland. One syllable. Bland. Food with very little flavor or taste. "The soup was a bit bland. It needed more seasoning." Bland.

**David:** Word three: tender. Tender. Two syllables. Soft, easy to cut, perfectly cooked meat. "The chicken was incredibly tender. It fell right off the bone." Tender.

**Alice:** "Fell right off the bone." Beautiful phrase. When meat is so tender, it slides off the bone easily. "Fell right off the bone." Very common food compliment for meat dishes.

**David:** Word four: refreshing. Refreshing. Three syllables. Making you feel cool, clean, energized. "This mint lemonade is so refreshing on a hot day." Refreshing.

**Alice:** Word five: savory. Savory, three syllables. Not sweet—salty, spicy, or herby flavors. "I always prefer savory snacks over sweet ones." Savory.

**David:** Phrase one: "on the side." Served separately, not mixed in. "Could I have the dressing on the side, please?" On the side.

**Alice:** Phrase two: "a hint of." A small, subtle amount. "I can taste a hint of cinnamon in this. What's your secret?" A hint of.

**David:** Phrase three: "melt in your mouth." When food is so soft and flavorful, it dissolves on your tongue. "This chocolate truffle just melts in your mouth." Melt in your mouth.

**Alice:** Phrase four: "What do you recommend?" A question to ask your server when you are not sure what to order. "Everything looks so good. What do you recommend?" What do you recommend?

**David:** And idiom of the day: "a piece of cake." Easy, simple, no problem. "Don't worry, it'll be a piece of cake." A piece of cake.

**Alice:** A piece of cake, but not the kind you eat.

**David:** Well, maybe that kind too, if there is dessert.

**Alice:** Always with the dessert.
